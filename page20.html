#307. Paris Saint-Germain sparkle on the pitch while the city of light burns around them | Barney Ronay

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

PSG, pet project of the state of Qatar, expresses the dualism of the city, a place of deprivation where the centre is perfect

Those who sought to bring down the Republic will have to answer for it.

Tell you who would have loved this? Who would have been utterly engrossed scrolling the hashtag #Paris on Tuesday night as two things happened simultaneously, the harrowing of the Barcelona defence at the Parc des Princes, and the harrowing of Paris itself via tales of city-centre thuggery and end-of-days political doom‑mongering.

The big man, Guy Debord himself. The Society of the Spectacle, you say? Actually, I think we’re past that now. Hold the society bit. We’ll just take the spectacle.

The great Parisian Situationist philosopher wasn’t really one for managed fun. But it was impossible not to be bowled over by the extraordinary pre-match show for Paris Saint‑Germain versus Barcelona, Champions League Matchday Three.

The wild pyrotechnics, the plumes of smoke, the full-wrap light show, the lime-green robo‑text spelling out every player’s name on the pitch. Although not the Barcelona names, sadly, for anyone with an unrealised desire to see the words “ANTHONY GORDON” projected in vast letters for the delight of a shrieking crowd.

Admittedly, the Halloween‑themed tifo banner showing a masked Paris Saint-Germain serial killer-type literally butchering the Barcelona badge to death with his bloodied chainsaw was perhaps a bit much. As were the Barça Puta signs, which are now reportedly the subject of a complaint to Uefa.

But it was a wild and all‑consuming spectacle, annihilating every other impulse in your head to a green thought in a green shade. And in the process capturing the profound sense of cognitive dissonance in the city.

Just walking around the streets of central Paris earlier in the day generated its own mild whiplash. On the face of it this was still the same city of flâneurs and tourists and everyday bistro business, and not, repeat not, a civil war drama or some kind of emergent military caliphate.

But the sense of dread was also tangible. You still got the occasional crunch of glass underfoot. Here and there charred bikes and scooters had been politely piled to one side. Even close to the Parc, away from the main hotspots, shopkeepers could be seen emerging warily into their doorways at the sound of a police siren.

By Tuesday night, a total of 2,513 people had been taken into police custody after the events of the weekend. At least 420 rental bikes had been destroyed or maimed. Liberated high-value retail goods were already being sold on Vinted, a commendable show of entrepreneurial energy from the maligned younger generation.

Not that you’d suspect these two worlds had any kind of overlap, lost in the booming triumphalism around PSG’s 4–2 win, one of the most beautifully luminous football matches you’re ever likely to see. Or indeed reading the coverage of an event that seems to have been treated in France like its own clear, clean space, floating above the flames, all the chat about the brilliance of Monsieur Dynamite, Désiré Doué, the wonderful television audiences on Canal+ TV, the whole of European football “kneeling before the machine” (are we sure this isn’t to do with anarchic rejection of social control?). “Paris est insubmersible!” was the headline in Le Parisien. But is it?

Remember when football used to reflect the world? Or just provide clues, a commentary, some kind of metaphor? This is no longer the case. At a time when even the most thunderous of commentators is struggling to summarise all this in suitably trite and damning terms, it was hard to avoid the feeling Paris Saint-Germain, the pet power football project of the state of Qatar, expresses this dualism perfectly.

Sometimes you just have to let football tell you things. “People who want to cause trouble in the city centres next Saturday will be prevented,” Emmanuel Macron announced on Tuesday evening, speaking, as it happens, from Doha, where he met Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad al-Thani, ultimate owner of PSG.

Macron is said to be considering deploying the French military to keep the peace on the streets before the next planned protests, although even here he may have to ask Qatar’s permission if he wants to deploy any of the French troops currently deployed working its missile systems, or embedded in the Doha security services.

The key event Macron is tooling up for is this Saturday night, 24 October, the date the gilets jaunes social media feed has set for an even greater swell of disorder. And again this will be football adjacent, with PSG playing Lyon at home on Sunday, a top‑of‑the-table capital‑city clash played out against whatever wreckage is left, however many thousands of Dot bikes are savaged, whatever guerrilla skirmishes are still playing out in the Marais.

What does this really have to do with football and the Ici C’est Paris brand appropriated by a state outreach project? Well, what are these demonstrations anyway? There have been many theories put forward as to what exactly is happening here, what is the spark, the kindling, the fire behind this disorder. School protests were the original line. Others have now roped in some kind of nebulous omni-cause, Instagram-activism, perhaps even a barbarian takeover, the decline of the west, savagery at the gates, of the type Donald Trump pretends to see.

Do you need a single reason? Take a look around. Modern life is pretty alarming on a minute‑to‑minute basis. Disaffection, alienation, a sense of being transformed in real time into a passive economic consumer unit, a set of eyeballs, an avatar of consumption. French youth unemployment is close to 20%. Robots nobody wants are being lined up to take jobs that don’t exist. Why don’t these young people want to join a youth club? Why don’t they sign up for the Liberal Democrats? Why are they enjoying empowered disorder? Maybe because nothing is yours around here, not even the things thrust into your brain as digital distractions. So why not grab something when you can? Debord would have called this “a vicious circle of isolation”, lonely crowd, technologies based on isolation.

On the other hand, what is PSG in Paris, other than the perfect expression of this? It often seems to surprise people that celebrations of PSG victories end up with riots and burnt-out cars. But Paris is both a beautiful and deeply dysfunctional city, a place of make‑believe and deprivation, where the centre is a perfect, performative relic, the suburbs shunted out to the ring roads, a life lived inside a box, noses pressed up against the glass, peering through your binoculars at the city of someone else’s love.

Paris is the divide between overclass and churn expressed in a single city. It’s everything you might have had but never will. And PSG represent this like no other entity, a football club owned as an act of outreach by a staggeringly rich inherited monarchy, weaponised passion, borrowed culture, engineered spectacle, deeply cynical but also deeply seductive.

Why are the Qataris splurging hundreds of millions into this project? Because they just really like high-pressing positional football? Qatar is the indentured labour state that employs Colin Kaepernick to promote its outreach projects. It will hymn its connections to the footballers of the banlieue, which is true and real and valuable, but not because of anything this political project wants or needs other than public relations.

It is in its own way a perfect human lab experiment, synthesised beauty, collectivism, with a cold, cold hand at its heart. And this is why there weren’t two versions of Paris in evidence on Tuesday night, just the same one, the dark side and the shiny side, an engine of the same divisions, the same kind of violence, right down to the sight of kids in PSG training gear dodging the police or taunting the barricades at the Place de la République.

And now we have Sunday night to come, more Ici C’est Paris, more PASSION. What kind of background will it be played out against? Does it matter under the lights? Will anyone notice?

Le Monde has described these events as a very basic test of the republic, its ability to bend, to remain essentially itself, the state’s willingness to pit itself against its own people. Football, as ever, holds these answers already. PSG’s Qatari owners have spent the past 15 years flaunting this city like a global luxury brand, extending its reach, creating its own ideal of conspicuous consumption, spectacle, triumph, son et lumière, bread and roses. As ever, if you want to know how and why, football has the answers.

#273. Manchester City have been a joy to watch but they became detached from their roots | Philipp Lahm

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

City behaved much like other top clubs but their rule-breaking deserves to be punished. The Bundesliga, where members have a controlling stake, can show the way forward

Do we have to reassess Manchester City now? In my columns, I have repeatedly engaged with this club and its key figures. Looking back at what I have written over the years, I believe it remains beyond question that the team have achieved greatness. I always loved watching them: they were a true unit on the pitch, a finely tuned orchestra.

I would also write all over again that the conductor, Pep Guardiola, masters his craft like few others. I could recognise his teams even if they were wearing the wrong shirts and the game was broadcast in black and white – by the passes, the distances between the players and the intelligence of their interplay. It is no wonder Guardiola inspires others to become managers; look at Vincent Kompany, Mikel Arteta, Enzo Maresca, and perhaps some time Ilkay Gündogan and Rodri.

As a player and a columnist, I never had an issue with Manchester City – precisely because, for many years, the club avoided buying the most obvious superstars. Guardiola said it himself: the most valuable of the valuable do not go to a club such as City, who aren’t even the biggest football brand in their own home town.

Many became world stars in Manchester. Kevin De Bruyne had been discarded by Chelsea, Rodri’s true class was overlooked in Madrid for a long time, Gündogan’s career was on the brink after injuries, and most recently Gianluigi Donnarumma was no longer wanted in Paris. In Germany, City signed players from Hamburg, Frankfurt and Wolfsburg. Erling Haaland in 2022 was the first player they targeted who was also pursued by Bayern Munich. City did not operate like Paris Saint‑Germain did in the era before Luis Enrique.

From the outside, City fundamentally did not do much differently from the other 10 or so top European clubs. But what are achievements on the pitch worth if they are accompanied by rule-breaking? The club have been found guilty of breaching the Premier League’s financial regulations between 2009 and 2018. It allegedly invested almost €1bn more than allowed and declared. The league leaders face potential forced relegation or titles being stripped. City continue to deny wrongdoing and are appealing against the decision.

It is right for the Premier League to crack down on unfair competition. I also appreciate that Germany is holding on to the 50+1 rule, which the federal cartel office recently upheld. It ensures that the majority of voting rights must remain with the parent club – the non‑profit section of a professional club. The exceptions that have applied to Leverkusen and Wolfsburg are under review.

The bedrock of our football culture is 50+1. This rule exists only in Germany, yet it speaks to a spirit that fits perfectly with the European sports model: 50+1 maintains a balance; on the one hand it allows for investment, on the other it guarantees participation. It strengthens the rights of members and fans. Perhaps it is because of this rule that the average attendance in Germany is higher than anywhere else in the world.

What is the ultimate purpose of football? It is a business, but its infrastructure is still developed by the general public. That is how I have always understood sport. Even as a professional, it was clear to me that my privileged life was made possible by many people working on a voluntary basis. Through my role as tournament director of the European Championship, these political connections have become even clearer to me.

The stadium and football clubs can remain the final places where people gather and society debates its issues only if the elite and the grassroots stay connected, and the public is involved in the process. Football must not be detached from its roots, as was the case with Manchester City. This becomes possible when rules are enforced by an institution. Ideally, not by a commercial entity but by a non-profit one such as Uefa.

In business, as in politics, I am observing a shift in mindset. Today, it is deemed necessary to invest in non‑profit institutions. More and more companies realise that football allows for a genuine exchange with the population – that it provides the perfect vehicle to set up corporate social responsibility projects.

This is what is needed in these politically turbulent times. In our fragmented world, political parties, churches and the media have lost credibility. In sport, however, people still come together. It is a tool to foster social cohesion. Therefore, I consider 50+1 to be a distinct competitive advantage for the Bundesliga.

I also welcome the fact that rules mean something in another case. Uefa is standing firm against Gianni Infantino, who recently wanted to privatise the World Cup and its profits. Along with his friend Donald Trump, he was seen by many as breaking the normal rules when Folarin Balogun’s suspension was lifted. The outcry from the global football community was loud, and it is possible that the Fifa president will fall.

When behaviour such as that in Manchester and at Fifa is punished, it is a good thing. Football must prevent questionable ideas from distancing it even further from its grassroots. Only then can it drive engagement in civil society, and only then can it strengthen Europe.

#245. Coach banned until 2048 for training children in DR Congo while suspended for sexual abuse

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Jonathan Bukabakwa gets extra five-year ban from Fifa

DR Congo’s FA must improve education and compliance

A coach in the Democratic Republic of the Congo who was banned from football for 20 years by Fifa for sexual abuse of a minor has had his ban extended by five years to 2048 by the world governing body.

Jonathan Bukabakwa, known as Messi, has also been ordered to pay an additional fine of $15,000 (£11,200) after the Guardian revealed he had continued to work with young players despite his suspension.

The Congolese Association Football Federation (Fecofa) and Racing Club Baki, where Bukabakwa was coaching, have been fined $50,000 each by Fifa’s ethics committee for failing to comply with its decision to ban him for 20 years in 2023.

Fecofa has also been instructed to introduce a “comprehensive education, compliance and monitoring programme for its officials, affiliated clubs and registered members” to try to ensure there is no repeat.

“The Congo DR Football Association, the club Racing Club Baki (formerly known as Esperanto FC), and the coach Jonathan Bukabakwa were found responsible for failing to comply with the decision rendered by the Fifa ethics committee on 18 October 2023,” said a Fifa statement.

Bukabakwa, who previously coached youth teams in the Limpopo and Malabo regions, was convicted of sexually abusing minors by authorities in the DRC and was released from prison last year. A Guardian investigation in August revealed there was video footage of him coaching young players at Esperanto in the Limete district of the capital, Kinshasa. Local sources confirmed he had regularly been seen taking charge of players on a school field.

Bukabakwa did not deny continuing to coach young players but claimed he had not been informed of his Fifa ban. He said he had not been questioned or received any communication from the governing body. He has denied the allegations against him.

The case has raised further questions about safeguarding in Congolese football and the ability of authorities to ensure that suspended individuals do not continue participating in the sport. Bukabakwa was provisionally suspended by Fecofa in November 2022 after media reports of alleged widespread sexual abuse in the DRC. Several other coaches were provisionally suspended at the time, although investigations into some of those other cases did not establish sufficient evidence for further action by Fecofa or Fifa.

Véron Mosengo-Omba, elected as Fecofa’s president in May, has promised to look into the previous cases once he has re-established a disciplinary committee. Fecofa was under a Fifa-appointed normalisation committee from 2023 until his election. “We will make sure that no one who is behaving like this will be involved in football in this country,” he said.

#330. Brighton are the apex B club – their next target should be a Champions League place | Jonathan Wilson

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Diego Gómez’s wondergoal in a five-star demolition is just the latest in an impressive run of results for the south coast team

For two or three years now, it’s been standard to nod approvingly at how well-run Brighton, Brentford and Bournemouth are, and to move on. What else is there to say? It’s true, of course – these are sides who have made the most of relatively limited resources. But there’s also an underlying assumption in the designation: that these are clubs who have no higher aspiration, that their limit is earning praise for upper mid-table finishes.

This season, perhaps, offers an opportunity to change that. Other than Arsenal, every major club is undergoing significant transition. Manchester City may have started the season with seven straight wins, but there’s every chance the punishment for their multiple breaches of Premier League regulations takes them out of the race for the top five. In that context, why shouldn’t one of those efficient smaller sides qualify for the Champions League?

Brentford began the weekend in third and with, perhaps, a slight sense of frustration that they had drawn four games. Again, they will feel they might have done rather better on Saturday; they were leading Liverpool 1-0 when Yehor Yarmolyuk foolishly flicked his head towards Jérémy Jacquet and was sent off just before half-time. The result was a 2-1 defeat and a sense of an opportunity missed. Bournemouth have not had the easiest start under Marco Rose, but Sunday’s 2-0 win over Sunderland perhaps hints at a brighter future.

Which left Brighton to carry the charge of the B clubs, which they did in emphatic style, ending a run of four derbies without success with a crushing 5-0 win. Crystal Palace had been extremely fortunate to survive a first-half battering from Nottingham Forest last week. They were thoroughly outplayed in the early stages again, and this time found themselves 3-0 down inside 10 minutes. Perhaps the opening goal, a cross headed into his own net by Jaydee Canvot, was fortunate, but it was an error induced by the intensity of Brighton’s start. The second goal, a perfectly weighted drive from just inside the Palace half from Diego Gómez, spoke of a side playing with immense confidence. Already this season, 11 different players have scored for Brighton in the league. They have become the image of a side without stars, the whole far greater than the individuals who comprise it.

The difference from February, when Brighton last played Palace, is stark. They lost that game 1-0, meaning they had won just one of their previous 12 Premier League games. Generalised grumbling had begun to curdle into calls for Fabian Hürzeler to be sacked. When they lost Carlos Baleba, Jan Paul van Hecke, Danny Welbeck and James Milner over the summer, there were reasons to wonder whether they could keep going with the process of continuous renewal.

There were the usual assorted signings, the usual mix of Premier League experience and exotic unknowns. The 19-year-old centre-back Luka Vušković, who spent last season on loan at Hamburg from Tottenham, already looks at home (and who really thought, when Spurs and Brighton effectively swapped centre-backs, with Van Hecke moving to London, that Brighton would not end up with the better of the deal?). The 21-year-old Spanish midfielder Chema Andrés, who arrived from Stuttgart, sparkled from the bench against Arsenal and has excelled in the two games since.

But the stand-out performer this season has been a far more familiar name: Pascal Gross, who returned in January after a season and a half at Borussia Dortmund. No player in the Premier League has registered more than his four assists this season. Nobody has run further. He has three goals. He averages a league-high 3.0 key passes a game. To bring back a player who is now 35 has been a masterstroke.

The victory over Arsenal before the international break suggested a team capable not merely of accumulating points against the league’s lesser lights, or even of challenging the elite, but of overwhelming them. Arsenal were suffocated by the Brighton press and, while a couple of low-xG long-range finishes in the first half helped, the fact is that Brighton dominated the match to a remarkable extent.

The question is whether they can keep it going. Just as their transfer policy is now an accepted feature of the Premier League, so too is Brighton’s mid-season slump. They haven’t won a game in December since 2023. Is this squad strong enough to sustain the challenge? Europe seems not to be unsettling Brighton as it did on their first foray, or as it appears to be draining Bournemouth, Crystal Palace and Sunderland – which may be a reflection on the relative quality of the Conference League.

It’s always a major step for a club unfamiliar with the Champions League to haul themselves into the top five. At times it often feels a mental barrier as much as anything.

But if any of those B clubs are ever going to break into the Champions League, they may never have a better opportunity than Brighton do this season.

#312. James McClean: ‘Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen’

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The former Republic of Ireland international on growing up in a community still reeling from the Troubles and the controversy that scarred his career

“The perception of me is that, basically, I am a madman,” James McClean says calmly. “I’m outspoken. I’m a loudmouth. I’m very opinionated and I hate all things British. That’s from people who don’t know me. The reality is quite the opposite. I’m pretty boring. I go training, go home and switch off. A lot of my days are quite bland, quite basic.”

McClean sounds as sane as he looks gaunt on a bleak Monday in Derry. He spent 14 years in English football and the fact that he is now at war with his own club, Derry City, can’t obscure the fact that he is the fifth-most-capped Republic of Ireland international, having played 103 times. “People define success in different ways,” he says, “but I’m the only footballer from the north of Ireland to win over 100 caps for Ireland. I played 150 Premier League games over five seasons, 800 professional games and won a lot of individual accolades. I count that as success and don’t look back with regrets.”

But there’s sadness, too, in the way his life and career were scarred by his refusal to wear the poppy when playing games around Remembrance Sunday in England. “It’s completely changed me as a person,” McClean says. “It’s made me a bit of a recluse because I don’t trust a lot of people now. I’ve been stung bad.”

McClean’s autobiography, The Walls, is one of the best sports books of the year. It’s a powerful and sometimes disturbing read elevated by McClean’s willingness to show himself in an unflattering light.

I have written a lot about Derry and boxing, and Bloody Sunday, but it helps to be back here to understand how McClean can respect the millions of soldiers who died in two world wars while refusing to forget how the British army killed so many Irish people in this city.

He comes from Creggan, the estate affected most on Bloody Sunday in 1972 when 14 unarmed civilians were killed by the British army in Derry. Six of the fatalities were from Creggan. In 2010, after the Saville report finally concluded that all the victims posed no threat, the British prime minister David Cameron apologised for the “unjustified and unjustifiable” massacre.

McClean was born in Derry in 1989 and he writes that “learning how to make a petrol bomb was one of my first life lessons. I was 11 before I’d properly mastered it.”

He pauses when asked how he would feel if any of his three daughters or his son, who is 11, ended up making petrol bombs. “I’m very thankful they don’t have to. It’s a different era now. I just want them to be proud to be Irish.”

The 37-year-old spells out the contrast with his youth. “I grew up in a very Republican community still dealing with the effects of the Troubles. You would see gunmen on the street. People got shot, kneecapped. To get access to my street you had to go through a lane where you sometimes saw masked men doing punishment beatings. But you weren’t scared. You knew you’d done nothing wrong. That was the norm … but it’s not normal.”

His biological father, Joe Kelly, was an alcoholic in Creggan and McClean spoke to him only once – when he shouted at him in the street to stay away. But he remembers a happy childhood and, when his professional career began with Derry City, “I was very carefree”.

McClean was 22 when Steve Bruce signed him for Sunderland in August 2011. He made his Premier League debut that December after Bruce was replaced by Martin O’Neill. McClean soon became a regular starter and that first season in English football was full of joy and praise. He was fast, skilful and disciplined. Apart from wasting money his biggest vice was typing his name on Twitter where he would revel in glowing reports of his play.

“It was amazing,” McClean says. “I never thought about the politics when I first got to England so up until November 2012, when the poppy thing started, I never experienced any bother.”

Everything changed when he told Sunderland he wouldn’t wear the poppy because it also represented the British army which had occupied Derry for so long. “There was never a decision for me to make,” he says. “Wearing the poppy was just never going to happen. It was a very difficult year and I felt a lot of that could have been handled better by the club. But Sunderland came out with a statement before the game which drew all eyes on me. It was there to protect the club. They basically said this is James’s personal decision but we, as a club, wholeheartedly support the poppy appeal.

“I wanted to make my own statement but I was constantly told by the club that: ‘No, no, it’ll die over. Just leave it.’ I’d just turned 23 and I thought the club had a duty of care to look after me and that’s what they were doing. I’d won the player of the year the previous season but I felt they hung me out to dry.”

McClean recalls that: “The next game was away to Fulham on a Sunday, on Sky. A threat was made that I would be shot if I came on to the pitch. I remember [his wife] Erin panicking. It sounds extreme and people might say: ‘Oh, wise up, you’re not going to be shot on TV.’ But the night before the game I was assigned two police officers to stand outside my door. So the Met police took it seriously and that made it more real.”

O’Neill brought him on in the second half and his every touch was booed – by the Fulham and Sunderland supporters. The vitriol poisoned his world but McClean kept searching for his name on Twitter. He became furious whenever, after a mutually respectful encounter with a member of the public, the person would go online to abuse him.

Sometimes McClean would track the person down and demand an apology. “Maybe that madness was always there, but it got stronger,” he notes.

McClean moved to Wigan before joining West Brom where, in “the best kind of madness” he earned £42,000 a week while he had his best spell in the Premier League. But as one poppy season blurred into another, the death threats kept coming, as did the warnings that Erin would be raped. McClean says: “My reaction was always to fight fire with fire. I went into some games in the wrong frame of mind. It wasn’t: ‘Can I help the team?’ It was: ‘I hope I score today so I can shush them up.’”

McClean admits: “There were days where I went looking for the conflict.” He posted a photograph of himself listening to his favourite Wolfe Tones song, The Broad Black Brimmer. “I knew exactly what I was doing,” McClean writes. “It was an IRA song – and I was sure it would rile a certain type of person up.”

By the time he reached Stoke, for whom he played 111 games between 2018 and 2021, his problems in English football reached a new low. “Brexit made the idiots louder” and “the poppy season seemed to be getting longer every year”. He had also made a stupid mistake during lockdown when he posted a photograph of himself, wearing a balaclava, with his two eldest children below a caption which read: ‘Today’s school lesson – History.’”

McClean thought he was being funny but his choice not to wear the poppy, in contrast, was heartfelt. “I thought others might have followed suit but it never turned out to be the case.”

Did he feel that a more rounded understating of his stance had emerged by the time he enjoyed his happiest seasons in English football, while playing for a Hollywood-tinged Wrexham from 2023 to earlier this year? “A wee bit, aye. When you explain it to footballers you’ve played with, and to people on a personal level, it opens their eyes and they say: ‘Oh, I understand that.’ But the overall British population? I don’t think so.”

Reflecting on his Premier League years, McClean marvels at the brilliance of Kevin De Bruyne and Eden Hazard. “You had to accept there were lads capable of doing things that were beyond you,” McClean writes. “But there were also games where I thought I was coming up against a physical strength that I found suspicious. Coming back into the Premier League after a break convinced me that some players had to be benefiting from some kind of performance-enhancing support.

“I remember coming up against one player that I’d encountered at Sunderland who I always felt that I’d been able to match in terms of his speed and power. While I’d been away from the Premier League, he’d gone to a bigger club, and now he was a different animal completely.”

These are startling allegations and, while McClean has no concrete evidence to support his doubts, he trusts his gut reaction. “I’ve always been very fanatical about my fitness,” he says. “But when you come against the top Premier League clubs, no matter what height, what size, they’re just faster, they’re stronger, and you’re thinking: ‘How is that possible?’”

After their daughter Willow was diagnosed with global development delay and autism, Erin persuaded him to be tested for autism. He was found to be “at the high-functioning end of the spectrum” but he still struggles with the fact that “I’ve probably got body dysmorphia or some kind of bulimia. Maybe it’s the autism thing, because I’m so strict with my diet 95% of the time. But when I can’t work out I binge. So if I’m travelling and flying, I’ll just eat that much shite to the point where I have to make myself sick. I still feel really guilty, because the next day I’ll punish myself in the gym – which is not healthy.”

Life has become even more complicated since he returned to Derry City in January. “I was meant to be coming home for the fairytale ending. But you quickly realise the promises are just bullshit.”

McClean has not played for the club since July and, asked about the situation, he says: “It’s not good. It’s a player against owner and there’s only one winner. I think that’s it in terms of Derry City. You never think you’ll be betrayed by your own so it’s painful.”

When contacted by the Guardian, a spokesman for Derry City said: “The club cannot speak to, or comment on, how James feels.” He also rejected the suggestion that Derry City had failed to fulfil any promises made to McClean.

Earlier this month, McClean said he couldn’t “understand or accept” the way he was being treated by the club and that he was fit to play despite being left out of the squad by interim manager Mark Connolly. Derry City then released a statement in which they said: “The club has sought to resolve this internal matter privately. Out of respect for confidentiality and the fact that this dispute involves sensitive medical details regarding James’ hip condition, the club have previously refrained from making any public statement on the matter.”

McClean insists he could play football elsewhere but how does he feel if this is the end of his career? “It doesn’t unsettle me because I’ve been boxing off and on for 10 years and in the past 18 months I’ve really thrown myself into it. I’m not going to start a pro boxing career because it’s just not feasible at my age. But I’ve been in contact with Misfits and they want me to fight.”

Misfits Boxing was set up by KSI and the Sauerland brothers, who made their name in the traditional fight game, and it features YouTubers and former athletes who want to become boxers. It is dismissed by boxing purists but McClean sounds enthusiastic about making his debut in the ring once his contract with Derry expires: “Boxing is giving me that purpose to wake up and train hard.”

What kind of boxer would he like to become? “I’m a pressure fighter. I like to be constantly in someone’s face because I’ve got the fitness to maintain that. I’m not saying I’m by any stretch of the imagination a [Gennady] Golovkin or a Canelo [Álvarez], but I love watching them. I’m not looking to have a career in boxing but three or four fights a year is perfect. The dream was always to be a footballer but this is a new excitement, a new challenge.”

As we prepare to step out into a rain-sodden Derry, there seems little chance of any reconciliation for him on the football field. But he looks up and his taut fighter’s face suddenly relaxes into a smile. “It could be a blessing,” McClean says softly. “It could end up a blessing in disguise.”

#360. Why are some football shin pads so small?

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Liverpool striker Lewis Koumas (second from left) sported low socks and small shinpads on his full Premier League debut at Anfield

Liverpool's Lewis Koumas made his full Premier League debut against Manchester City on Sunday.

While doing so, he sported some very low socks over what appeared to be a very small shin pad.

Last season, images of Burnley's Marcus Edwards went viral after the 27-year-old was seen wearing barely visible shin pads during a Premier League match against West Ham.

Two years ago, a picture of teenager Harry Howell celebrating with James Milner while they were Brighton team-mates also gained attention after fans noticed the visible difference in the size of their shin pads.

Howell's 'micro' pair were an obvious contrast to Milner's far more traditional-sized guards.

So what is behind the trend of extra-small shin guards, and why are they used instead of regular-sized pairs?

The simple answer is that players can choose what size shin pads they want to wear.

The International Football Association Board (Ifab), responsible for the laws of the game, say that shin pads are a mandatory piece of equipment players must wear while playing.

Be of an appropriate size to provide reasonable protection and be covered by the socks

And players are responsible for the size and suitability of their shin pads

The law does not define what an "appropriate" shin pad size is, but leave that decision to each individual player.

Shin pads provide vital protection for players to prevent serious injuries.

There has been a rising trend in younger footballers, as well as some big stars, wearing small shin pads during matches.

The reason for this could be for more comfort and to allow players to move around with greater ease.

Referees are only required to check that some form of shin guards are being worn, Ifab told BBC Sport.

Ifab said players - or the parents or guardians of youth players - must take responsibility for their own safety on the pitch.

While shin pad size is ultimately an individuals choice, any decisions around player safety would also be made in consultation with their club and medical staff.

Marcus Edwards last year was seen wearing very small shin pads for Burnley in the Premier League

Grassroots football teams have started to ban small shin pads for safety reasons.

There are concerns that small shin pads do not cover the shins properly, which can lead to an increased risk of injury - especially at non-elite level.

In August 2024, a football club banned small shin pads after a youth player suffered a double leg break.

Penistone Church player Alfie Collins broke his tibia and fibula in a tackle during a game. The teenager's shin pads, which were no bigger than a credit card, were similar to a size recently worn by some stars of the professional game.

Shin pads "play an important role in injury prevention as they are intended to protect the shin from injury", the Football Association said.

"'Micro' or 'mini' shin pads may therefore increase the risk of injury due to the smaller amount of shin covered compared to larger sized shin pads," they added.

#289. Next Generation 2021: why slow and steady sometimes wins the race

📅 Sep 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Our NextGen picks from five years ago show that breaking through early does not guarantee longevity or future success

Read the full updates on our class of 2021 here and here

In 2021, as every year, we picked 80 players from around the world who had a chance of making it in world football – 20 from the Premier League and 60 from the rest of the world – as part of our Next Generation series.

We then follow these talented boys on their journeys for five years. Often it is almost impossible to predict how they will get on (OK, when Sid Lowe sent over Lamine Yamal in 2024 we had an inkling he might do well).

Five years ago we had an exciting array of young players on the list, most of them 17 years old but some 16, with one youngster from Brighton being profiled like this:

double quotation markThe ball-playing central midfielder comes from a footballing family and academy staff believe his tactical awareness is born from growing up around the game. His father, Adam, played for Brighton and is now manager along the coast at Worthing FC, while Martin (his great uncle), Paul (his grandad), and Wally (his great grandad), all enjoyed careers as players.

It was quite an understated entry, especially compared with this one in the world section:

double quotation markThe youngest to play in the Bundesliga at 16 years and one day, youngest player to appear in the Champions League 17 days later, youngest goal scorer in Bundesliga 11 days after that and youngest player and youngest goal scorer for the Germany Under-21 team before he has turned 17.

Borussia Dortmund’s Youssoufa Moukoko went on to play for Germany at the World Cup the following year, at the same time as Jack Hinshelwood was promoted to the Brighton Under-21s. Different trajectories, different pressures. Moukoko was already known around the world and tipped for a long career at the top. There was something completely carefree about his style, young Michael Owen-esque. Take a look at his early (and subsequent) goals in the Bundesliga here.

Then life interfered. Moukoko sustained some injuries and struggled to make the Dortmund starting XI. To be fair, he also had some decent competition up front as Erling Haaland had arrived in 2020. Moukoko had a really good spell at the start of the 2022-23 season, after Haaland had joined Manchester City, but sustained an ankle ligament tear and never quite got his starting place back.

These days Moukoko is playing in the Turkish top flight with Corum, on loan from Copenhagen, where he moved in 2025. His switch to the newly promoted Turkish top-flight side was low key with the club welcoming Moukoko to the “family” with a short statement. He is yet to start a game but his cameos off the bench have offered flashes of his quality – most notably marking his home debut with a sharp assist in a 3–0 win over Eyüpspor. His market value is €5m (£4m) reckons transfermarkt.com.

Meanwhile, Hinshelwood has made 67 Premier League appearances and was included on Thomas Tuchel’s 55-man longlist for the 2026 World Cup. A frustrating ankle injury has hampered him at the start of this campaign but his star is rising and he has a market value of €28m (£24m). Not that Brighton are about to sell him.

Neither player chose their path and whether they broke through early. They simply developed at a different pace. Hinshelwood was not ready to play top-flight football at 17; Moukoko was. Did the early exposure have a negative impact on Moukoko? It is impossible to say.

Not that it has been straightforward for Hinshelwood either. It never is. His father, Adam, told Sky this year that “it is definitely not just a straight line”, saying: “There were lots of bumps and ups and downs along the way.” There was one moment when Hinshelwood, then with the under-15s, was set a challenge to play with an older age-group, and that arguably made him as a footballer. “It was a big catalyst for Jack,” Adam said. “He rolled up his sleeves and became a proper footballer.”

After that elimination, Chandler developed a superstition of never again giving pre-match interviews - as the last time he did so was before the 4-0 defeat.

Whether because of his renewed focus, or because of a desire among players to make amends, it steeled the group to make history.

"It's the hardest thing to keep a team together - as soon as players get noticed, they get picked up at higher level," Chandler tells BBC Sport.

Ian Chandler played for Barnsley, Stockport and Aldershot before a successful non-league career

Despite being a lifelong Sunderland fan, Chandler admits basing his team on Kevin Keegan's Newcastle - with entertainment even placed above winning.

"There was a standing joke in the team, two or three at the back? One time we chased a game with two at the back," he says.

"The Premier League can be so boring; fans want to be entertained, that's what we promised."

Chandler was already a Whitley Bay hero when he was appointed manager by chairman Paul McIlduff, having scored the winner in the 2002 FA Vase final against Tiptree United at Villa Park.

After a slow start as the then-35-year-old with no prior managerial experience found his bearings, Chandler built a talented team which became the envy of non-league football nationwide.

"We played good football. We had pace on the wing, fantastic midfielders, and strikers who were deadly," says McIlduff, who has been Whitley Bay chairman since 2001.

"When we brought them in, it was about fitting the psychology of the dressing room. No-one thought they were better than anyone else, they played for each other."

"He was my manager at work and football, I was sick of him," he tells BBC Sport. "But it helped get time off for matches."

"Zoning out happened regularly, picturing scoring and winning, what the opposition would be like - hoping they were rubbish so we could have an easy stroll," he says.

"Everyone goes to the finals, you were there, you could enjoy it," says Robinson, who is now academy head at South Shields.

"One quarter-final against Biggleswade [in 2009], they turned up in suits and ties," says Robinson. "We thought, this is their day out, their cup final. We won 5-2.

Paul Robinson scored the fifth goal in Whitley Bay's 6-1 win over Wroxham in the 2010 FA Vase final

Off the pitch, the team's success had an influence on the whole of Whitley Bay. A working-class town near Newcastle, which had its heyday as a Victorian-era holiday destination, sporting success brought local pride to the north-east coast.

And like any lower league club, that success could only be supported by dedicated volunteers, who went into overdrive before the Vase finals.

They would go door to door across shops in Tynemouth, North Shields and Whitley Bay to raise support. A van selling tickets was parked outside Woolworths on the high street.

"There was such excitement around the whole of Whitley Bay - every shop had things in the windows, the blue and white stripes," says one of the volunteers, Jenn Robinson.

She got involved because her late husband Mike was a club board member and if she wanted to see him she "had to go around there".

"Everyone wanted to come and watch, we got so many local sponsors," adds Jenn.

"I still do buffets and things for players and long-standing fans. We have a game tonight, and I'm making the sandwiches now."

Newcastle United helped out with selling tickets for the finals, while North Tyneside Council provided security services as thousands flocked to Hillheads Park for big cup games.

But even the best laid plans can go awry.

"We organised a train down to London, the guarantee was that it would go straight to Wembley," says McIlduff. "The first time it worked, second time they were taken to Kings Cross - we had to organise taxis up to Wembley."

By hook or by crook, The Seahorses army made it three times to the national stadium. Some even got royal treatment.

"It was absolutely surreal, and a delight," Jenn says. "The first Wembley final we played at, it had just opened. My husband being on the board, we sat in the royal box, which was fabulous.

"One of the security guys showing me to our seats said, 'Miss Robinson, this is Prince William's seat'. He hadn't even sat in it at that point."

Thousands of fans travelled from Whitley Bay to London across the three finals

Chandler, whose son Benjamin was a mascot at all three finals, said it was an overwhelming experience at times for the players too.

"The first year was crazy, everything was brand new," he says. "The first year the bus driver scraped the side up of the bus driving into the ground.

"The second year we tried to take everything in, all the lads had their mobiles. Then the third year, it was just a case of can we do it, win three in a row."

And three times, Whitley Bay rose to the occasion.

After beating Glossop North End 2-0 in 2009 in front of 12,200 supporters, Whitley Bay returned a year later to thrash Wroxham 6-1.

The opening goal was scored by prolific striker Paul Chow after only 21 seconds. At the time this was the quickest goal in a Wembley final and was only surpassed 13 years later when Ilkay Gundogan scored for Manchester City inside 13 seconds in the FA Cup final.

The hat-trick was completed in 2011 with a frantic 3-2 win over Coalville Town, who equalised twice before Chow was again the hero with an 86th-minute winner.

In total, nearly 30,000 fans were in the stands across the three finals.

After each final, Whitley Bay celebrated in The Green Man pub just 15 minutes' walk from the famous Wembley arch.

"In the first year we just went back to the bar and it was an anti-climax," Robinson says. "The second and third years the club got their act together and we rented a room; families, friends and fans were invited, they were brilliant nights.

"In 2010, we actually spent 10 days together - the week of the final we spent as a squad preparing, then we went to Marbella for five nights. Best 10 days of my life!"

The 2010 trophy lift was perhaps the most emotional though, as the players were joined by former team-mate Mark Taylor. He had played in the previous year's final, before being diagnosed with motor neurone disease, forcing an early retirement.

"Mark used to come on the away trips after he got diagnosed, he just wanted to be Mark - we had a good laugh everywhere we went," says Chandler. "He's still knocking about, abusing me on Facebook. He was the core of our second and third year."

Whitley Bay lift the trophy in 2010, joined by former team-mate Mark Taylor (left)

"One year I got a standing ovation when I walked into the office, but after that it was just back to normal," he says.

"People wind you up about it - 'are you talking about Wembley again? Give it a rest Robbo!"

But the memories in the football-mad north-east remain.

"Me and my wife went out on a Sunday at a pub in Durham just a few weeks back, and this guy came up out the blue and asked, 'Are you Ian Chandler?'" he laughs.

"Then he started talking about that team; in the north-east, everyone follows football. It was so lovely."

It was, says Ido Rakovsky, like reporting on "science fiction", rather than sport.

Israel, whose senior team have qualified for the World Cup just once, had beaten global powerhouses Brazil in the quarter-finals of the under-20 version of the tournament.

At the final whistle, the commentator covering the match for Israeli television vowed "you will tell of this night to your children, and they will tell it to your grandchildren".

Five days later, the team's semi-final against Uruguay was the most-watched thing of the evening on Israeli television, drawing a 23% viewing share.

Politicians clamoured to congratulate the team.

"That summer, everybody was watching under-20 football, everybody knew the players," says Rakovsky, who was covering the team for Israeli media outlet Haaretz.

"Everybody in Israel fell in love with these kids."

It wasn't just their deeds on the pitch that felt otherworldly, though.

After scoring a stoppage-time winner against Uzbekistan in the last 16, Anan Khalaili had given an interview to Israeli television.

In it Khalaili, an Israeli Arab, thanked the viewers back home for staying up late to watch his team win in Argentina.

To do so, he used the words 'Am Yisrael' - a Hebrew phrase that means 'People of Israel' and is bound up in Jewish religion, community, culture and history.

"That an Arab player used that figure of speech said something about the identity of that team," says Rakovsky.
