#41. Howe, Nagelsmann, Pochettino: can anyone end Manchester United’s doom loop?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Even an elite coach would surely struggle at Old Trafford amid the executive chaos and with an unfit, uneven squad

In January 2014 the 13th series of Celebrity Big Brother aired in the UK, the show so popular that its run time was extended by five days before a public vote anointed Jim Davidson the winner. Then, two years later when Brexit happened, people wondered where their tolerant country had gone, lamenting the ephemeral spirit of London 2012 as though the 2011 riots were some kind of fever dream, not a desperate response to decades of structural and institutional racism exacerbated by the cruelty of unnecessary austerity.

The truth is that if security is quickly upended, it was built on quicksand to begin with. Which brings us to Manchester United, easing effortlessly from an uplifting third-place finish to some of the worst football in their history.

It is entirely possible that Alex Ferguson made a deal with the devil to the effect that United would be great until he retired and hilariously dreadful thereafter. But assuming not, his l’état, c’est moi mentality was busy setting up the post-him fall even at the height of the success – itself a consequence of what had gone before.

A day after the Munich air disaster, Louis Edwards – later a seller of condemned meat to schools – joined the board, eventually assuming control thanks to a dodgy rights issue. He bequeathed the club to Martin – his substellar son – who floated it for personal gain, turning an institution that sought to win, entertain and unify into a business legally compelled to return value to shareholders.

This left United vulnerable to acquisition by anyone and, when Ferguson fell out with two directors – a horse, a horse, his kingdom for a horse’s ejaculate – they sold to the Glazers, who loaded the purchase price and accordant interest obligation on to the club, with government and football authorities waving through a move since outlawed. Ferguson, meanwhile, must have feared exactly what the takeover would entail and known that without his support the banks might not have lent the money, while the chief executive David Gill admitted opposition to it but stayed in his role anyway, and so the pillaging began.

The English game then exploded, with investment in United left available only to those of wealth so extreme it necessarily casts aspersions over their character – such as arch polluter and anti‑immigration zealot, Jim Ratcliffe. Proclaiming inaccurate and divisive rhetoric contrary to the inclusive, cosmopolitan spirit of Manchester and United, jobs were culled and fights picked with fans, Old Trafford’s most desirable seats socially cleansed so the club could sell them to day-trippers for astronomical prices, with longstanding supporters banned after trumped-up charges of touting – a deeply principled stance the club fails to apply to itself.

Not untypically for a billionaire, Ratcliffe seemingly believes that correct opinions reflect exchequer not expertise, giving himself final say on footballing decisions. And rather than the “best in class” executives he promised, his United operate according to what political analysts call the “Boris principle”, no‑marks in thrall to their own self‑image given jobs for which they lack the ability and character, the consequent chaos being effective sabotage.

So the patterns continue: Champions League qualification is missed, insufficient funds are released; a new unsuitable manager spends heavily on players below requisite standard; Champions League qualification is achieved via unsustainably reactive style; the manager is denied necessary funds amounting to less than the bounty of Champions League qualification; the manager imposes a proactive style without the necessary materials; the manager is belatedly fired for a fee far exceeding that saved by cutting jobs; a new unsuitable manager, with entirely different requirements to his predecessor, is appointed; executive accountability is absent.

It is thus no surprise that Michael Carrick remains unfired, despite exuding the vibe of a man who’s mentally fired himself. Perhaps it’s a stay of execution, but more likely there’s bizarre hope of a turnaround, partly to save paying him off, partly because the relevant executives can’t achieve consensus on a replacement, and partly because surely even Omar Berrada and Jason Wilcox accept that, following another catastrophe, after dismissing Carrick they are honour-bound to dismiss themselves.

Except they and United don’t work like that.

So, just as they failed Carrick by not recruiting properly; failing to add experience to his coaching team; failing to replace outgoing fitness and set-piece coaches and failing to interrogate details of his new game-model, they are now failing him by having him continue in a job that is too much for him, destroying a season that remains salvageable. Meantime, the Glazers’ dividends continue unabated.

As such, the question isn’t so much who should be United’s next manager as can anyone manage United? Increasingly, it looks like the answer is – to quote Don Logan in Sexy Beast – “No”.

There are immediate on‑pitch issues: the team are unfit and their collective mentality weak; after years being lied to, the captain has finally broken; and the players hate the suits, the package toxic. Those are the minor problems.

Roughly, football management comprises five aspects: coaching, tactics, talent ID, pastoral care and presence. And, though no manager excels in every department, at this version of United surely they must. Moreover, given this is no job for a novice, track record is also important – and it might be here that Unai Emery’s candidature falls down, his brilliance in transforming mid‑ranking clubs undermined by failures at two high-profile basket cases.

Which brings us to pastoral care and presence. In the postwar era, only Erik ten Hag has won a trophy at United without tangible charisma, the size of the club now demanding it more than ever. This is not a quality associated with Eddie Howe, while Antonio Conte does not scream pastoral care and, brilliant though his career has been, he failed miserably at Spurs, the only environment he has inhabited that is remotely similar to United.

Should United miss Champions League qualification, financial strictures will make an elite coach and tactician essential. But in any event, football has changed such that the alchemic art of combining players and personalities no longer guarantees success.

Which leaves us with talent ID, player quality and intelligence being the best coaching and tactics there are. Of the 78 players purchased post-Ferguson, only Bruno Fernandes and perhaps Zlatan Ibrahimovic could be classed as cast-iron successes, so the next man – assuming United don’t target Emma Hayes, who embodies more of the right stuff than almost anyone else – must have a keen nose. Even Jürgen Klopp, the other manager who fulfils these criteria – and of all the damage Ed Woodward wreaked on United, turning him off was most costly – benefited from a department which delivered the greatest run of transfers ever.

Which leaves Mauricio Pochettino, who built something at Tottenham despite the environment, and Julian Nagelsmann. But had Pochettino not signed a new contract with the USA men’s team, the sense is that, as when Mourinho arrived at Old Trafford after years of speculation, he’s past his magical best, while Nagelsmann may be the free-thinking genius of his own imagination, just as he may be an emperor’s new clothes bluffer.

All of which is to say that failure has many fathers and, once ingrained, is not easily eradicated. Yet just as society has too much going for it to ever move quite beyond the pale, so too does Manchester United. Even the longest journey starts with one step; but does anyone have the nous to ensure it’s taken in the correct direction?

#158. Expectation vs reality: What if Man Utd have become a mid-table team?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Michael Carrick remains under pressure at Man Utd after a poor start to the season; former midfielder is club's eighth permanent boss since Sir Alex Ferguson retired in 2013 but this week's Between the Lines explains why they have bigger problems

Michael Carrick has been given a stay of execution by Manchester United after the club considered making a fourth managerial change in four years following their defeat by Bournemouth.

Carrick, the club's eighth permanent boss since Sir Alex Ferguson's retirement in 2013, came under major scrutiny after their poor start to the season continued with the 3-0 loss at Old Trafford.

But what if United aren't an elite team underperforming - what if they are in fact a mid-table team whose results have made them look better than they are in the post-Ferguson era?

What if the managers aren't really to blame?

United's managers have paid the price for the club's struggles over the course of the last decade, but research for Between the Lines shows they have mostly overperformed in terms of points won in the Premier League relative to 'expected' points.

United's total of 669 points is the fifth-highest in the Premier League in that timeframe. But the Sky Sports expected points model, which factors a team's expected goals and expected goals against by game to determine the most likely outcome, puts United on 613, meaning they have taken 56 points more than expected.

In the Sky Sports expected table, a team is awarded three points when outscoring their opponent's xG by more than 0.5, one point if the margin is between -0.5 and 0.5 xG, and no points if their opponent outscore their xG by more than 0.5.

By way of example, although United beat Chelsea 1-0 at Stamford Bridge on April 18 last season, the xG was in Chelsea's favour by 1.57 to 0.29, meaning the 'expected' outcome was a United defeat.

#400. Life as a Wag in the transfer window

📅 Aug 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

"Owen was on the phone to the manager and his agent while I was in labour. They waited for me to give birth and then he went out on loan pretty much as soon as I was out of hospital."

Owen Dale's loan deal wasn't to a local club, but to Portsmouth - a city more than 200 miles away. His partner, Jessica Beaumont, had never even been there.

And the pair had only just got the keys to a house they had bought. They would end up staying in it for just one night.

"Since living together, we've moved five times in five years," says Jessica.

"You have to be prepared for anything in the transfer window. But it's also a really fortunate position to be in at a young age."

This period - when clubs are permitted to buy, sell and loan players - takes place twice a year, in January and during the summer, for clubs in the Premier League, the English Football League (EFL) and the Women's Super League (WSL).

While a select few stay in one place throughout their career, for most players moving from club to club is just part of life in the modern game.

Jessica met Owen when he was at Crewe Alexandra. A move to Blackpool soon followed.

"I didn't get together with him because he was a footballer so I never considered the footballer life - that he might just have to move at the last minute," she says. "We just met and we clicked."

But it soon became apparent that dealing with almost constant uncertainty would be part of that life.

The move to Blackpool was a loan, with an option to buy, so for six months - during which Jessica was pregnant with their son Xavier - they did not know whether he would be staying there or heading back to Crewe.

Blackpool decided to sign Owen, but he was soon out on loan again. This isn't uncommon, with loan deals a way for clubs to help young prospects gain first-team experience and get regular playing time elsewhere.

"When the move to Portsmouth happened it really hit me - I had to quickly realise that 'OK this is now my life'," says Jessica.

Just before Owen's loan deal to Portsmouth the couple had bought their first house together in Congleton - a town close to Jessica's family in Stoke on Trent, and commutable to Blackpool.

"We spent one night in there - we hadn't even unpacked," she says.

They decided a 10-hour round trip on Owen's day off in the week to see his newborn son was not an option.

"We thought 'if he's not playing well because he's tired, what's the point in doing this'?" says Jessica, who adds that they ended up renting out the Congleton house and have been living in rented accommodation themselves ever since.

"I was finishing my law degree and had a job at the time - I had to say to them I wouldn't be coming back from maternity leave," she says.

Post-natal complications also made it difficult for her to travel at the time.

"I had Owen on the phone basically saying, 'I've done a couple of house viewings. I've chosen this one. This is where we're going to be living' and showing me on FaceTime," she recalls.

"In that moment I did feel really out of control."

Uprooting her life at short notice and leaving her family behind was challenging and Jessica remembers spending a lot of time alone in Portsmouth when Owen was at away games.

But she remembers lots of positives from that time too, including when she became friends with another player's partner.

"I was so fortunate that she would often stay with me when Owen was away," she says.

"I say to her all the time that I'll appreciate that time forever because I was just so isolated.

"Our schedules were the same - when Owen went away her partner was also away - I didn't have to be fully alone and she got it."

Owen made 28 appearances for Plymouth Argyle last season

Owen, now 27, has played most of his career in League One, with two stints in the Championship when we was with Blackpool and Oxford United.

Last season was spent on loan at Plymouth Argyle and he is currently a free agent - this means he is not tied to any club by a legal contract. Owen can now join a new team without a transfer fee because no former employer owns his registration rights.

Many of his moves have been on deadline day and he says he is "very aware of the sacrifices" his family make.

"There's always the risk that a move might not work," he says. "So I might uproot my whole family for this next venture and it all goes south.

"Over the years I've learned that until there's a contract signed on the dotted line, you could end up anywhere."

For now, Jessica's career in law is on hold, she instead has a job in social media and works from home.

"My career can be picked up at any time - whereas his career is right now and you've really got to take every opportunity," she says.

"As much as people say it's a glamorous lifestyle you do make sacrifices so your partner can have their dream and get themselves as far as they can in football.

"At the end of the day, as much as I'm involved, it's his career. That means in order for me to support him properly I can't get emotionally involved."

This includes not getting too emotionally attached to where they live.

"We usually rent newer houses that are a blank canvas so we can make them our own, although this last season the accommodation was provided furnished by Argyle," she says.

"It can be hard when he asks us to get things like his trampoline which we don't have with us."

Owen's career is always the priority, Jessica says.

"When Owen was at Oxford he had one year left on his contract and I loved it there," she says.

"But the best thing was for him to go and get game time somewhere miles away. So we moved."

While Premier League footballers can earn tens of thousands of pounds a week - sometimes hundreds of thousands - life in the lower leagues can be very different.

"When you think 'Wag' [players' wives and girlfriends] and you think 'footballer', both of those terms come with this stereotype that our lives are easy," Jessica says.

"People also put League One players in the same bracket as Premier League players.

"In the Premier League they're often set up for life - we can't live in that way because we have to prepare for life after football."

Adding to the uncertainty of where they will be playing is also the possibility or relegation or injury.

"When we were at Portsmouth, Blackpool was still his parent club and they got relegated," says Jessica. "Owen wasn't even involved that season, but his wage was still affected.

"You have to be prepared for everything. You just can't live outside of your means.

"But it's still a really fortunate position to be in.

"Luckily, Owen's been good with injuries. There are some players who they get injured in the last month of the season and they're out of contract.

"In football things can be really positive or really negative."

The uncertainty also affects their children - four-year-old Xavier and two-month old Etta.

"Now that Xavier is growing up - he's fast approaching school age - another thing that will have to be added into the mix is his stability," says Jessica.

Even normal things like organising his recent fourth birthday party aren't always straightforward.

"We couldn't plan to have a party in Plymouth with balloons and cakes etc because we didn't know if we were going to be there," she says.

"Instead we planned a party near my family in Stoke-on-Trent - we needed to make something work regardless of what happens with Owen's job.

"As much as I worry about Xavier and Etta, I also think how amazing it's going to be for them when they're older to be able to tell their life story and say they've lived in all these different places.

"I think it's teaching them a good life lesson that they don't need to be tied to one place."

She says they have made every move a positive experience.

"We've said to Xavier that we might be moving soon and when we do we're going to get his bike back out of storage," says Jessica.

"That way he knows that moving is a possibility and hopefully he's not thinking about what he's going to lose but what he's going to gain.

"The fortunate thing for us is that Xavier loves football. He gets a lot of excitement as well from the new strips and the new chants and all of that kind of thing."

Xavier has enjoyed watching his dad play at each of his new clubs

So, does Jessica have any tips for people in a similar situation?

"The main thing for me personally - and what I've learned first hand - is the importance of keeping your own identity outside of football," she says.

"If you get too wrapped up in it, it only puts more pressure on your partner.

"If you can get a hobby, a routine or even a work-from-home job, that can help - things that can move with you."

Being sensible financially is also important.

"I'd also say that if you have a season where you get promoted, try not to change your lifestyle and to bank the promotion money so that you have as much stability as you can," she adds.

"In our house, I de-clutter maybe once a month," she says. "This helps me because I don't have control over Owen's career. But what I do have control over is making sure that if and when anything does happen, the house is ready to be packed up and moved.

"When it's the transfer window I will plan in one-week blocks - that gives me that little bit of certainty in a situation where I have no control."

She admits "it can be hard" but "on the flip side it's so exciting".

"We've lived in places that we never would have lived in. And that's exciting for a relationship," she adds.
