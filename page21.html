#396. 73 goals from dropping deep - we ask Kane to break down how he did it

📅 Sep 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Harry Kane is one of the leading names in the race for this year's Ballon d'Or, having scored 73 goals last season for club and country

As Harry Kane's career has progressed, the 33-year-old has taken an increasingly complete role, popping up to collect the ball as deep as his own box.

Many wonder why a talisman tasked with scoring doesn't spend more time closer to goal, but last season the England and Bayern Munich striker scored 73 goals - the highest tally of his career - while playing deeper than he ever has done.

When I mention this paradox, a grin spreads across his face.

"Until you really understand football and understand what we've been talking about, it's so easy to say 'stay high, you're going to score' but it doesn't work like that," Kane says.

I sat down to speak to Kane about the finer details of his game, shedding light on his unique role.

Watching Kane train, his professionalism is palpable. England's record goalscorer is one of the first out on the pitch. The younger players indulge in playful kick-ups as he watches, engaged in conversation with a member of staff.

From a distance, that makes it easy to confuse him with a coach but, given his role on the pitch, it is a fitting mistake.

Kane's style of play reflects who he is as a person, with those who know him saying he is well-rounded and humble on and off the pitch.

The season is in its infancy but Kane isn't basking in the success of the past 12 months. With only five days of pre-season training under his belt, the England captain completed 90 minutes of Bayern's 2-1 win over Borussia Dortmund to lift the German Super Cup.

As demanding as his roaming role is, he has taken the physical demands of it in his stride. Across the past six seasons, STATSports - a sports technology company Kane has invested in - found his distance covered, high-speed running and accelerations were up 8%, 22% and 42% respectively.

And given how the tactical landscape has changed over that time, it is particularly impressive Kane has been able to not only keep up, but stay ahead.

Kane's role has evolved drastically over the past nine years, with the striker now heavily involved in all phases of the game -Â including as deep as his own box

"Over the last five or six years, man-to-man pressure has become bigger," Kane says.

"European football is physical and top teams are trying to win the ball back high, trusting their defenders and midfielders to defend man for man to win their battles."

Given that change, the best teams have recruited physical athletes and brilliant dribblers who can beat their man when the game becomes a series of one-against-ones.

Kane, not known for his dribbling - and by his own admission "not the fastest", has thrived on his own terms.

Against Dortmund, there was a specific moment Kane dropped deep, looking to arrive in space unmarked. Recognising the danger, two players converged on the Englishman.

Kane's dribbling is cerebral. His speed of thought provides him solutions to problems others would attempt to solve with their feet alone.

After getting low and using his strength to shrug centre-back Waldemar Anton off him, Kane rose, slightly adjusting his body to the right. These micro-adjustments are a hallmark of his game and through them he is able to shift an entire team's momentum.

Fooled by this feint, Daniel Svensson and Jobe Bellingham found themselves wrong-footed as Kane snapped the ball back the other way, opening up the entire pitch from within Bayern's half.

Kane wins his individual battle with Waldemar Anton, shielding the ball and showcasing strength

As he rises, he positions his body to the right, facing his own goal.

Through clever body positioning, Kane moves the opposition towards the right side of the pitch. This is his cue to cut the ball back into the open space

This allows Kane to evade two defenders, now facing the opposition's goal

Earlier this year, Bayern boss Vincent Kompany shed light on Kane's unique role, explaining it as being 50% coached and 50% Kane deciding what to do.

"I think it's partly instinct, using my football brain and getting the feeling on the pitch," says Kane. "If, for example, I want to drop and see if the centre-back really wants to commit, he might commit into midfield, but is he going to commit if I drop all the way to my box?

"After the first five or 10 minutes you really get an understanding of how the opposition are going to play, which dictates how I play."

In the second minute of Bayern Munich's game against Arsenal, Kane dropped deep into a position normally occupied by centre-backs. Arsenal centre-back William Saliba marked him all the way, vacating space higher up the pitch

There is a shared understanding at Bayern of an approach that allows team-mates to make sense of rotations that, to the naked eye, appear random.

Kane, who is given more freedom than most, is at the centre of it.

"I feel like my position now is as one of two 10s, and you work together," he says. "You can double up on one side to overload, which causes the opposition centre-backs a problem of who to mark.

"If they want to mark both, they've got to go man-to-man and then that leaves space for our wingers or full-backs to run into.

"It's posing all these questions to the opposition but, personally, I love having someone close to me who I can link with."

Kane built his reputation playing as a lone number nine, so it is interesting to hear him identify instead as a number 10 -Â in a front two.

"When you're the number nine on your own you can get marked by two centre-backs and you struggle to get touches on the ball," he says. "You're relying on everyone else to find you with the ball."

Showing Kane two clips from last season, I ask him to talk through what was going through his head.

"The thing with our formation is, as long as there's someone in each position, it doesn't really matter who it is. Lucho [Luis Diaz] in this clip is playing as one of those 10s.

"I've come off to try and create a bit of space and Lucho's gone through the centre."

Distracted by Kane's dropping-deep movement, one of the opposition midfielders is pulled towards him. This creates space, allowing Joshua Kimmich to punch the ball through to Diaz.

"I think it's Serge [Gnabry] making the run in behind which takes the defenders back, which allows me and Lucho that space in between. Lucho lays it off and whenever I'm in that position, around 20-25 yards out, I'm always looking if I can get my shot off."

It is one of many examples of Kane initially dropping deep before scoring.

"By playing deep when the play develops, I end up coming into a number 10 spot when everyone's dropped," he says. "I can get shots off from the edge of the box, which is a big strength of mine, or I'm running off the back of players where no-one's picking me up - because the midfielder finds it hard to [track]."

With Frankfurt's central midfielder pulled towards Kane, Luis Diaz is able to receive a pass between the lines. He forms a central front two with Kane. The wingers ahead of them, Serge Gnabry and Michael Olise, push the defensive line back.

Kane is found in space around the edge of the box and strikes the ball hard into the net

The second clip is from Bayern's Champions League tie against Club Brugge.

Usually Kane is marked by an opposition centre-back, but the Belgian club give that responsibility to their holding midfielder -Â something he recognises and exploits.

Lennart Karl scores without Kane touching the ball, but the striker's involvement is undeniable.

"These are the little runs, again, not everyone sees," Kane explains. "With Jamal Musiala and Lenny, they're so good at receiving the ball and dribbling so there'll be times where I will actually stay higher and let those guys get on the ball a bit deeper, and I'll try and make runs in behind.

"Without changing my game too much, I try and complement the players around me.

"I know Lenny is so good in those pockets and when he gets there, I'm more thinking of running in behind because, one, he can play me in and I can go through on goal, or two, he can dribble with the ball and do exactly what happened here.

"As soon as Lenny gets in that pocket I make the run and not always to get the ball, sometimes it is just to drag the defender away.

"Now he's one-against-one with a centre-back in the middle of the pitch and that's dangerous for any centre-back."

Karl receives the ball in the middle of the pitch. As seen in yellow, Kane is being marked by Club Brugge's holding midfielder, rather than any of the central defenders

With the Ballon d'Or announcement on the horizon, Kane's name is in the conversation, primarily for the sheer volume of goals he has scored. What is easier to miss in that debate is the rest of his game.

Kane plays for the team in each phase of the game across the entirety of the pitch, while at the same time producing extraordinary individual numbers that match the likes of Cristiano Ronaldo, Lionel Messi and Erling Haaland.

He has described himself as playing in the role of a number 10, nine and six at times.

In a football world of specific and heavily drilled tactical roles, that is quite the achievement.

An 18-year-old who has played at two major international tournaments and the division’s top clearance maker from last season also feature

A tricky, exciting, quick and versatile midfielder, Azeez has proven himself in the Championship with Millwall over the past two seasons and now deserves a chance to test himself in the top flight.

Brighton had been chasing him for a while and struck a deal on deadline day for a very reasonable reported initial £12.5m, rising to £15m. If he can make the step up and produce anything like his second-tier form, he’ll be a steal.

He is a high-volume producer at the sharp end of the pitch, constantly making things happen that threaten the opposition’s goal, but standing out most for his crossing. He ranked top of the Championship last season for open-play crosses (227) and expected assists from crosses (6.71 xA).

He also ranked fourth for dribble attempts (131), ninth for shots (95), sixth for chances created from open play (58). He created good-quality chances, too; only two players created more big chances than he did (18), and he ranked fourth for expected assists. His 9.12 xA alongside only seven assists suggests he may benefit from playing alongside better finishers.

A hugely exciting prospect. Despite being 18, Mbaye has significant and noteworthy experience under his belt. Last year he made six appearances, including in the final, for Senegal at the Africa Cup of Nations and this summer he made four appearances at the World Cup. He has also played in two Ligue 1 title-winning campaigns, including 2024-25, when he became the youngest player in PSG’s history to start a game, and won the Champions League in both of those seasons

He is a dribbling and ball-carrying menace, who always looks as if he’s about to do something special. Among players to play at least 900 minutes in Ligue 1 last season, he was 11th for carries (moving at least five metres with the ball) per 90, with 20.0, and 12th for progressive carries (moving at least five metres upfield with the ball) per 90, with 10.3. Most of the players above him in both lists are defenders, who naturally have more space to move into.

He was third for take-on-ending carries, or running with the ball for at least five metres before attempting to beat an opponent with a dribble, with 2.9 per 90. And he also has end product to boot. He scored at Afcon and the World Cup despite limited minutes and his 0.45 goals or assists per 90 for PSG last season is not to be sniffed at.

Danso had a pretty impossible task over his two years at Tottenham. He was the back-up to the first-choice centre-backs Cristian Romero and Micky van de Ven, playing intermittently on both sides of central defence in dysfunctional teams jumping between styles of play, and he very rarely played for any consistent run of games.

He was unfairly compared with the players he was replacing for much of his time there, but the truth is he was always reliable enough. He excelled, however, when Romero got injured during the run-in last season and he was called upon in Spurs’ time of need as they scraped clear of relegation.

He may not be the best on the ball – though he is hardly terrible – but he is an excellent deep defender. Among those to play at least 1,000 minutes in the Premier League last season, Danso ranked first for clearances (8.2 per 90) and headed clearances (6.3), and was fifth for aerial duels won (4.8 per 90).

He could thrive at Sunderland, who spent more of their out-of-possession game time in a low block than any other team in the Premier League last season (25%). Danso could be perfectly at home in this setup, paired alongside the player he beat into second place for clearances and headed clearances: Dan Ballard.

It was always said during Pep Guardiola’s time at Manchester City that attackers had their wings clipped in terms of individual freedom, limited to playing within the parameters of the manager’s meticulous gameplan.

But dribbling ability became increasingly important in the final years of his reign, with Jérémy Doku and Antoine Semenyo on the wings and Rayan Cherki in between. Doku (162) and Semenyo (144) ranked first and second for dribbles attempted in the Premier League last season, with Cherki ninth (111). They also ranked first (84), fourth (59) and eighth (51), respectively, for dribbles completed.

With the deadline-day addition of Ndiaye, City have gone in even harder on dribbling ability under Enzo Maresca. The Senegal international ranked second for dribbles attempted (136) and dribbles completed (73) in the Premier League last season.

Fernandez-Pardo’s versatility is a major attraction. He’s comfortable playing off the left and cutting in on to his right foot, or as a No 9. Although not the bulkiest, he has explosive pace and promises to be a threat in behind.

The Belgium international is arguably at his most threatening when running with the ball because he does so with such purpose. His 122 carries progressing the ball at least 10 metres upfield in Ligue 1 last term were bettered by only seven players, several of whom were defenders.

On top of that, he ranked top for carries into the penalty area (69) and seventh for carries ending with a shot or chance created (39).

Fofana appeared to be enjoying a swift rise in 2024-25, only for a serious ankle injury to massively disrupt his development in 2025-26. Sunderland will be hoping they can get him back to the levels shown the season before last, when he was a real livewire on Lyon’s left flank.

He contributed nine goal involvements (five goals, four assists) in 1,586 minutes in Ligue 1 in 2024-25, averaging one every 176 minutes. Only eight forwards (900+ mins) averaged more expected assists per 90 (0.25).

The most eye-catching aspects of his game, however, were his dynamism and pace. He could be difficult to stop once moving, and this made him a very useful outlet. Among Ligue 1 forwards (900+ mins), his 16.6 carries per 90 ranked him sixth, and he moved up to third for progressive carries per 90 (11.5).

Fofana is direct, exciting and has significant potential. He is a player Sunderland could make a profit on if he reaches the levels previously expected.

Andrés arrived to bolster Brighton’s midfield options after Carlos Baleba’s exit to Manchester United. Although a different type of midfielder to the Cameroonian, Chema is another with vast potential.

A product of Real Madrid’s academy, Chema joined Stuttgart last summer. During his sole campaign in the Bundesliga, he was in and out of the team but caught the eye at the base of their midfield.

Comparisons have been made to Rodri because of his physical attributes and nominal role as a holding midfielder. He doesn’t have anything like the same influence in possession but Chema is tidy on the ball.

It’s what he offers defensively that is most notable, however. He’s been broadly praised for his anticipation and reading of the game, ranking second among midfielders (900+ mins) since the start of last season for interceptions per 90 in the Bundesliga (2.3).

Looking after female referees within Northern Ireland, trying to recruit, retain and give them experience within their games to pushthrough into top level refereeing.

#400. Life as a Wag in the transfer window

📅 Aug 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

"Owen was on the phone to the manager and his agent while I was in labour. They waited for me to give birth and then he went out on loan pretty much as soon as I was out of hospital."

Owen Dale's loan deal wasn't to a local club, but to Portsmouth - a city more than 200 miles away. His partner, Jessica Beaumont, had never even been there.

And the pair had only just got the keys to a house they had bought. They would end up staying in it for just one night.

"Since living together, we've moved five times in five years," says Jessica.

"You have to be prepared for anything in the transfer window. But it's also a really fortunate position to be in at a young age."

This period - when clubs are permitted to buy, sell and loan players - takes place twice a year, in January and during the summer, for clubs in the Premier League, the English Football League (EFL) and the Women's Super League (WSL).

While a select few stay in one place throughout their career, for most players moving from club to club is just part of life in the modern game.

Jessica met Owen when he was at Crewe Alexandra. A move to Blackpool soon followed.

"I didn't get together with him because he was a footballer so I never considered the footballer life - that he might just have to move at the last minute," she says. "We just met and we clicked."

But it soon became apparent that dealing with almost constant uncertainty would be part of that life.

The move to Blackpool was a loan, with an option to buy, so for six months - during which Jessica was pregnant with their son Xavier - they did not know whether he would be staying there or heading back to Crewe.

Blackpool decided to sign Owen, but he was soon out on loan again. This isn't uncommon, with loan deals a way for clubs to help young prospects gain first-team experience and get regular playing time elsewhere.

"When the move to Portsmouth happened it really hit me - I had to quickly realise that 'OK this is now my life'," says Jessica.

Just before Owen's loan deal to Portsmouth the couple had bought their first house together in Congleton - a town close to Jessica's family in Stoke on Trent, and commutable to Blackpool.

"We spent one night in there - we hadn't even unpacked," she says.

They decided a 10-hour round trip on Owen's day off in the week to see his newborn son was not an option.

"We thought 'if he's not playing well because he's tired, what's the point in doing this'?" says Jessica, who adds that they ended up renting out the Congleton house and have been living in rented accommodation themselves ever since.

"I was finishing my law degree and had a job at the time - I had to say to them I wouldn't be coming back from maternity leave," she says.

Post-natal complications also made it difficult for her to travel at the time.

"I had Owen on the phone basically saying, 'I've done a couple of house viewings. I've chosen this one. This is where we're going to be living' and showing me on FaceTime," she recalls.

"In that moment I did feel really out of control."

Uprooting her life at short notice and leaving her family behind was challenging and Jessica remembers spending a lot of time alone in Portsmouth when Owen was at away games.

But she remembers lots of positives from that time too, including when she became friends with another player's partner.

"I was so fortunate that she would often stay with me when Owen was away," she says.

"I say to her all the time that I'll appreciate that time forever because I was just so isolated.

"Our schedules were the same - when Owen went away her partner was also away - I didn't have to be fully alone and she got it."

Owen made 28 appearances for Plymouth Argyle last season

Owen, now 27, has played most of his career in League One, with two stints in the Championship when we was with Blackpool and Oxford United.

Last season was spent on loan at Plymouth Argyle and he is currently a free agent - this means he is not tied to any club by a legal contract. Owen can now join a new team without a transfer fee because no former employer owns his registration rights.

Many of his moves have been on deadline day and he says he is "very aware of the sacrifices" his family make.

"There's always the risk that a move might not work," he says. "So I might uproot my whole family for this next venture and it all goes south.

"Over the years I've learned that until there's a contract signed on the dotted line, you could end up anywhere."

"My career can be picked up at any time - whereas his career is right now and you've really got to take every opportunity," she says.

"At the end of the day, as much as I'm involved, it's his career. That means in order for me to support him properly I can't get emotionally involved."

This includes not getting too emotionally attached to where they live.

"We usually rent newer houses that are a blank canvas so we can make them our own, although this last season the accommodation was provided furnished by Argyle," she says.

"When Owen was at Oxford he had one year left on his contract and I loved it there," she says.

While Premier League footballers can earn tens of thousands of pounds a week - sometimes hundreds of thousands - life in the lower leagues can be very different.

"When you think 'Wag' [players' wives and girlfriends] and you think 'footballer', both of those terms come with this stereotype that our lives are easy," Jessica says.

"People also put League One players in the same bracket as Premier League players.

"In the Premier League they're often set up for life - we can't live in that way because we have to prepare for life after football."

Adding to the uncertainty of where they will be playing is also the possibility or relegation or injury.

"When we were at Portsmouth, Blackpool was still his parent club and they got relegated," says Jessica. "Owen wasn't even involved that season, but his wage was still affected.

"You have to be prepared for everything. You just can't live outside of your means.

"But it's still a really fortunate position to be in.

"Luckily, Owen's been good with injuries. There are some players who they get injured in the last month of the season and they're out of contract.

"In football things can be really positive or really negative."

The uncertainty also affects their children - four-year-old Xavier and two-month old Etta.

"Now that Xavier is growing up - he's fast approaching school age - another thing that will have to be added into the mix is his stability," says Jessica.

Even normal things like organising his recent fourth birthday party aren't always straightforward.

"We couldn't plan to have a party in Plymouth with balloons and cakes etc because we didn't know if we were going to be there," she says.

"Instead we planned a party near my family in Stoke-on-Trent - we needed to make something work regardless of what happens with Owen's job.

"As much as I worry about Xavier and Etta, I also think how amazing it's going to be for them when they're older to be able to tell their life story and say they've lived in all these different places.

"I think it's teaching them a good life lesson that they don't need to be tied to one place."

She says they have made every move a positive experience.

"We've said to Xavier that we might be moving soon and when we do we're going to get his bike back out of storage," says Jessica.

"That way he knows that moving is a possibility and hopefully he's not thinking about what he's going to lose but what he's going to gain.

"The fortunate thing for us is that Xavier loves football. He gets a lot of excitement as well from the new strips and the new chants and all of that kind of thing."

L'aspettano a braccia aperte, ma prima bisogna rinunciare alla Super Lega, un progetto "stupido", secondo il presidente dell'Eca, Nasser Al Khelaifi che però spalanca le porte alla Juventus. E in attesa di una sentenza europea sui monopoli Uefa e Fifa, rimane lo stallo tra l'associazione dei club europei, guidata dal qatariota, succeduto proprio ad Andrea Agnelli, quando due anni fa quest'ultimo fece secessione, puntando tutto sul campionato privato d'élite, insieme a Real Marid e Barcellona, gli unici club rimasti a sognare un'altra Champions League.

Per il presidente del Psg Al Khelaifi comunque non ci sono tabù, come ribadito ieri al termine della riunione dell'Eca: “Se smettono di fare quel che considero sia un progetto stupido saranno sempre benvenuti”. Un messaggio chiaro, dunque, per rientrare nella famiglia Eca che in stretta collaborazione con l'Uefa ha respinto l'assalto della Super Lega privata, finalizzata a creare una sorta di campionato transnazionale chiuso, dove solo le big avevano garanzie sportive e soprattutto economiche. Un piano orchestrato da Real Madrid e Juventus, con il supporto del Barcellona, le uniche società rimaste dopo che i tifosi costrinsero i principali club inglesi a ritirarsi, come altre squadre italiane, tra cui le milanesi. L'apertura di Al Khelaifi però non è stata colta dal club torinese: "La Juventus – ha fatto sapere il servizio stampa bianconero all'agenzia Reuters – non valuta la possibilità di tornare nell'Eca, per il momento". Almeno fino alla sentenza della Corte di giustizia europea che il 21 dicembre dovrà esprimersi proprio sui monopoli di Uefa e Fifa nell'organizzazione delle competizioni sportive.
